The 23 components you need

TLDR. Learn these 23 well and you can design any small-business-plus-spike prompt. They're in the order you use them in the interview. Each card shows a diagram, what the part is, an analogy, a real scenario with and without it, the line to say, and the related terms as tiles. Everything else is in the optional reference; skip it for now.

One-sheet view: all the cards plus a worked framework (PDF), 2 landscape pages.

Study plan

  • Day 1: cards 1 to 10. Then draw the pizza-shop V1 from memory.
  • Day 2: cards 11 to 18. Then answer 2 sample questions out loud.
  • Day 3: cards 19 to 23 and the numbers. Then 2 more questions and 10 drills.

Draw these in V1

1

CDN

Servers around the world that keep copies of your site's files (pages, images, scripts) and serve each visitor from one nearby.

Local warehouses stocking your product, so not every order ships from the factory.
Every designFirst spike defenseFiles that rarely change
Scenario. A bike shop's site gets shared on a forum: 50,000 visitors in an hour, about 2 MB each.
Without it
  1. Every page, photo and script comes from the shop's 2 servers
  2. They max out, and pages crawl
With it
  1. First visitor per region: 1 fetch from the shop
  2. Everyone after: served by a nearby CDN server
  3. Shop servers only see cart and checkout
Why it works: The files are the same for everyone, so don't make your servers send them every time.
"The CDN takes most of the read spike before it reaches us."
DNSThe internet's phone book: pizza.com to a server address.
Low DNS TTLSet it before launch, so you can redirect traffic fast.
2

Load balancer

Sits in front of 2+ identical copies of your backend, hands each request to one, and skips any copy that stops answering.

A traffic cop sending cars to open lanes.
Every designSurviving a crashed copy
Scenario. A tire shop's booking site runs 2 copies. At 9 AM, one crashes on a bug.
Without it
  1. Only one copy: the site is down
  2. Every customer sees errors until someone restarts it
With it
  1. Health check gets no answer within seconds
  2. All traffic goes to the healthy copy
  3. Restarted copy passes the check and rejoins
Why it works: One crash shouldn't take the whole site down.
"Two copies behind a load balancer, so one can die."
Health checkThe 'are you alive?' ping, every few seconds.
HTTPSEncryption usually ends at the load balancer.
Reverse proxySame idea: a server in front that forwards requests.
3

Stateless API, one service

Each copy of your backend forgets the user between requests. Carts, orders and logins live in the database, so any copy can serve any request.

Bank tellers: any teller can help you, because your account is in the bank's system, not in one teller's head.
Every designScaling by adding copies
Scenario. The pizza shop goes from 2 copies to 8 an hour before kickoff.
Without it
  1. The cart lives in copy 1's memory
  2. The customer is stuck on copy 1; new copies sit idle
  3. Copy 1 crashes: the cart is gone
With it
  1. Copy 1 saves the cart to the database
  2. The next click lands on copy 5, which reads it back
  3. All 8 copies are useful immediately
Why it works: Stateless copies are interchangeable, so more copies means more capacity.
"One service, run as several identical copies. Stateless, so I scale by adding copies."
MonolithOne program deployed as a unit. Right for a small team.
MicroservicesMany small programs calling each other. For big orgs.
Managed containersThe cloud runs copies of your packaged app (Cloud Run, ECS, Container Apps).
KubernetesYour own cluster for that. Needs people to run it.
ServerlessCode that runs once per event. Fine for small glue.
4

Postgres (managed)

The standard relational database: tables of rows that many users can change at once without corrupting anything. Your source of truth.

A shared spreadsheet that enforces its own rules.
OrdersPaymentsStockBookings
Scenario. Two customers click the 9 AM Saturday slot at the tire shop at the same instant.
Without it
  1. Booking saved, but the bay never marked taken (crash in between)
  2. Both customers get the same bay
With it
  1. One transaction: mark bay taken and save booking, both or neither
  2. Unique rule on (bay, time) rejects the second insert
  3. Second customer is offered the next free slot
Why it works: These facts must never be wrong, and the database guarantees them. A few writes a second is nothing for one database.
"Postgres, not DynamoDB: my write changes two rows together, and one database is plenty at these numbers."
TransactionSeveral changes succeed or fail together (ACID).
ConstraintA rule the database enforces, like unique (bay, time).
IndexA sorted lookup, so a query doesn't read every row. Every frequent query needs one.
ManagedThe cloud handles backups, patches and recovery.
DynamoDBHuge-scale lookups by id; clunky multi-row changes; ~1,000 writes/s per hot item.
ShardingSplitting data across databases. Never in V1.
5

Object storage

Cloud storage for files, each saved under a name. Cheap and practically unlimited. The database keeps only the file's name.

Google Drive for your app.
PhotosPDFsVideosExports
Scenario. A print-shop customer uploads 10 photos (4 MB each) for holiday cards.
Without it
  1. 40 MB per order passes through the API servers
  2. Thousands of orders clog servers and bloat the database
With it
  1. API hands the browser 10 signed upload links
  2. Browser sends photos straight to storage
  3. Database keeps the names; a worker fetches photos to print
Why it works: Storage is built for big files; your servers and database aren't.
"The browser uploads straight to storage with a signed URL, so photos never pass through our servers."
Signed URLA temporary link to upload or download one file directly.
CDNServe stored files through it.
6

Queue + workers

A to-do list for slow work. The server writes a note and replies at once; background workers pick up notes and do the work.

Order tickets on a kitchen rail: the cashier doesn't cook.
EmailsTextsImage processingAnything that can lag
Scenario. Each paid pizza order sends a confirmation text. The SMS provider takes 1 to 2 s and sometimes times out.
Without it
  1. The customer waits on the text before seeing 'confirmed'
  2. A provider timeout becomes a checkout error
With it
  1. API queues 'text order 42' and confirms instantly
  2. A worker sends the text, retrying on failure
  3. After 5 failures: dead-letter queue and an alert
  4. 900 orders in 30 min: texts lag minutes, none lost
Why it works: Checkout stays fast, and a slow provider can't break ordering.
"At-least-once delivery, plus workers that are safe to run twice."
At-least-onceA note may be handed out twice, so jobs must be safe to repeat.
Dead-letter queueWhere a note goes after N failures. Alert on it.
Queue lengthAdd workers when the list gets long.
KafkaA replayable event log for many readers. Overkill here.
Pub/subOne message delivered to several listeners.
OutboxSave the message in the same transaction as the order.
7

Sweeper and reconciliation jobs

Programs on a timer that clean up. The sweeper releases stuck things. Reconciliation compares your records with an outside source and fixes gaps.

Closing-time checks: count the till against the receipts.
Expired holdsStuck ordersMatching payments
Scenario. 50 pizza customers reserve, then abandon the Stripe page. One paid order's webhook never arrives.
Without it
  1. Abandoned pizzas stay reserved: lost sales
  2. The paid order is never cooked
With it
  1. Every minute, the sweeper finds expired holds
  2. It asks Stripe 'paid?'; if not, the pizzas go back on sale
  3. Nightly: compare Stripe's payments with our orders, fix the missing one
Why it works: Some messages always get lost. These jobs make the system end up correct anyway.
"Every minute, release expired holds, after asking Stripe whether they were paid."
CronThe classic name for 'run this on a schedule'.
One runnerOnly one copy runs at a time, so sweepers don't fight.

Protect the invariants

An invariant is a rule that must never break, like "never sell more pizzas than the ovens can make".

8

Conditional update

Check and change in one database command: 'subtract 1 only if at least 1 is left'. The database runs it as a single step.

Grabbing the last item off a shelf: only one hand can take it.
No oversellStockSeatsTime slots
Scenario. 1 TV left on sale. Alice and Bob click Buy in the same millisecond.
Without it
  1. Both read 'left = 1'
  2. Both write 'left = 0'
  3. 2 TVs sold, 1 in stock
With it
  1. Both run the UPDATE below
  2. Alice: 1 row changed, left = 0
  3. Bob: 0 rows changed, sees 'sold out'
UPDATE stock SET left = left - 1
WHERE id = 7 AND left >= 1
Why it works: The database refuses to go below zero, no matter how close the clicks.
"One statement, so the database refuses to go below zero."
SELECT ... FOR UPDATELock the row for complex logic; others wait.
Redis counterAvoid: then two places hold the truth.
Distributed lockAvoid: the database already does this.
9

Holds that expire

Reserve the item first, then take payment. Unpaid holds expire. The status only moves forward: held, then paid or expired.

A store setting an item aside for 10 minutes while you get your wallet.
Anything reserved before payment
Scenario. A customer picks 2 pizzas for the 6:00 slot and clicks checkout.
Without it
  1. Charge first, then find the slot is full
  2. Refunds and angry customers
With it
  1. Reserve 2 of the slot's 150: held, expires in 10 min
  2. Pays on Stripe: webhook flips it to paid, kitchen gets it
  3. No payment in 10 min: sweeper releases the 2
Why it works: You only take money for pizza you can actually make.
"Hold, then charge, so we never take money for something we can't deliver."
State machineThe fixed statuses and allowed moves between them.
Forward onlyA late Stripe message can't turn paid back into held.
10

Idempotency key

A random id for each user action. The server saves it with a uniqueness rule; if it arrives again, it returns the first result instead of repeating.

A numbered ticket: show it twice, get served once.
No double chargeEvery create or chargeEvery webhook
Scenario. A customer on a train taps 'Place order'. The order is created, but the reply is lost in a tunnel.
Without it
  1. The app retries
  2. The server creates a second order and charges again
With it
  1. The app retries with the same key, a7f3
  2. Server finds a7f3 and returns the original order
  3. Order id also sent to Stripe as its key: one charge
Why it works: Retries are unavoidable on real networks. Without the key, each one is a possible double charge.
"A client key with a unique constraint, the same key passed to Stripe, and webhook event ids deduped."
IdempotentDoing it twice has the same effect as doing it once.
Unique constraintThe rule that rejects duplicates. Also stops double booking.
Webhook dedupeSave Stripe's event id and ignore repeats.
Exactly onceNot achievable. You get 'at least once, repeats harmless'.

Survive the spike

11

Cache with a short TTL

A small, very fast memory (Redis) holding recent answers. The TTL is how long a copy lives before it's fetched again.

A sticky note with today's specials, rewritten every 2 seconds.
Slots leftMenuCatalog
Scenario. Game day: 20,000 people refresh 'slots left', about 500 requests a second.
Without it
  1. 500 identical database queries every second
With it
  1. First request every 2 s reads the database and saves to Redis
  2. Everyone else gets the Redis copy in under 1 ms
  3. Database load: 1 query per 2 s
  4. Stale '3 left'? Checkout's conditional update says sold out
Why it works: 2-second-old numbers are fine for browsing; the sale is still decided by the database.
"Cached for 2 s. It's a hint; the database is the truth."
TTLTime to live: how long a cached copy is kept.
InvalidationDeleting copies on every change. Harder than a short TTL.
StampedeA hot copy expires and thousands hit the database. Let one refresh.
Hot keyOne item everyone reads. Also keep a 1 s copy in each server.
12

Rate limits

A cap on how often one person or machine can call you. Extra requests get an instant 'slow down' error.

A bouncer letting each person through once a minute.
BotsRetry stormsProvider limits
Scenario. A reseller's script hits checkout 300 times a second from one IP. A buggy app retries in a tight loop.
Without it
  1. The script floods servers and buys the stock
  2. The retry loop turns a hiccup into an outage
With it
  1. Edge cap of 10/s per IP: 290 rejected before the servers
  2. Business cap: 1 per customer per item, across IPs
  3. The retry loop gets 'slow down' errors, not the database
Why it works: It protects real customers' fair share, and the servers.
"Per IP at the edge, and 2 orders per phone in the API."
429The 'too many requests' error code.
WAFWeb application firewall: blocks known attacks and bots.
DDoS protectionAbsorbs floods of junk traffic. WAF and DDoS come with the CDN: mention them, don't design them.
13

Waiting room

A 'you're in line' page. Visitors wait there and are let into the real site at a fixed rate. You buy it from a vendor.

A rope line outside a busy store.
Demand far above capacityA sale that starts at an exact minute
Scenario. A sale starts at exactly 6:00 AM. 40,000 people are refreshing; checkout was tested for 300 a second.
Without it
  1. 40,000 requests at once; the site falls over
  2. The fastest connection wins
With it
  1. Everyone lands on the line page
  2. Everyone who arrived before 6:00 is shuffled
  3. Let in 300 a second; each sees their place
Why it works: The site runs at a load it was tested for, and the order is fair.
"Behind a flag. On only if traffic beats the plan."
FairnessShuffle everyone who arrived before the start.
VendorsCloudflare Waiting Room, Queue-it.
14

Replicas and failover

The primary database takes all writes. Replicas are live, read-only copies a fraction of a second behind. A standby takes over if the primary dies.

A co-pilot ready to take the controls.
More read capacitySurviving a database crash
Scenario. Heavy sales reports slow down checkout. Later, at 2 AM, the primary's machine fails.
Without it
  1. Reports compete with checkout for the one database
  2. A hardware failure means hours of downtime
With it
  1. Reports read from a replica; checkout stays fast
  2. The cloud promotes the standby in 30 to 60 s
  3. Writes in that minute fail, and the app retries them
Why it works: Replicas take read load off the one writer, and the standby turns a failure into a one-minute blip.
"Never read stock you're about to sell from a replica. It can be a moment behind."
FailoverPromoting the standby when the primary dies.
Multi-AZStandby in another data center, same region. Always do it.
Multi-regionA full copy far away. Only if an hour down is unacceptable.
Point-in-time restoreRewind to any minute, to undo a bad delete.
RPOHow much recent data you could lose.
RTOHow long you'd be down.
15

Connection pooler

Postgres handles only a few hundred connections. A pooler shares a small set of real connections among all your server copies.

A few phone lines shared by a whole office.
As soon as you add copies
Scenario. Before the game, the pizza shop scales from 2 to 20 copies, 20 connections each.
Without it
  1. 400 connections against a limit of 200
  2. Copies 11 to 20 can't connect and fail at kickoff
With it
  1. All 20 copies connect to the pooler
  2. The pooler keeps 50 real connections
  3. It lends one out for the milliseconds a query takes
Why it works: Scaling out the API multiplies connections unless something shares them.
"Postgres allows a few hundred connections. Without a pooler, scaling the API takes down the database."
PgBouncerThe classic pooler.
Short transactionsNever wait on Stripe while holding one.
16

Pre-scaling

Add server copies and upgrade the database before a spike you know is coming.

Calling in extra staff before the lunch rush, not during it.
Any planned event
Scenario. The snow forecast airs on the 6 PM news. Tire-shop traffic will jump 20x in 2 minutes.
Without it
  1. Autoscaling notices the busy servers at 6:06
  2. New copies are ready at 6:09
  3. 4 minutes of errors at the worst moment
With it
  1. 5 PM: 2 to 6 copies, bigger database, CDN warmed
  2. 6:05: the spike arrives; the system is already sized
Why it works: You know when the spike is coming, so use that.
"Autoscaling reacts in minutes. The spike arrives in seconds."
AutoscalingThe cloud adds copies when servers get busy. Keep it as a safety net.
WorkersScale them on queue length, not CPU.

Call third parties safely

17

Hosted checkout + webhooks

Customers pay on the payment provider's page. The provider then calls your server (a webhook) to say the payment worked.

Letting the bank's own teller handle the cash.
Every payment
Scenario. Order 42 ($24) at the pizza shop.
Without it
  1. Your own card form puts card numbers on your servers
  2. Heavy security audits (PCI)
With it
  1. Create a Stripe Checkout session; the customer pays there
  2. Stripe calls /webhooks/stripe: 'order 42 paid'
  3. Check signature, save event id, mark paid, reply OK fast
  4. Same event again later? Known id: ignore it
Why it works: You never touch card numbers, and the webhook tells you about a payment on someone else's page.
"Card data never touches our servers, which shrinks our PCI scope."
WebhookThe provider calling your URL when something happens.
Webhook rulesCheck the signature (proof it's Stripe), reply OK fast, do the work later, ignore repeats, expect some late.
PCISecurity rules for handling card data.
Test modeFake payments, for load tests.
Rate limitAbout 100 requests/s by default. Ask for more before launch.
SMS, emailSame pattern: call from a worker, respect the limits.
18

Timeouts, retries, circuit breaker

Three rules for any outside call: give up after N seconds, retry with growing waits, and stop calling for a while after repeated failures.

Hang up on hold music, call back later, and stop dialing if the line is clearly dead.
StripeSMSAny outside service
Scenario. Game day: Stripe calls slow down from 300 ms to 8 s.
Without it
  1. Each checkout ties up a server for 8 s
  2. Every server waits on Stripe; even the menu stops loading
With it
  1. 2 s timeout: fail fast, retry once with the same key
  2. 5 failures in a row: the circuit opens for 30 s
  3. Checkout says 'try again in a minute'; the rest stays fast
Why it works: One slow outside service shouldn't take down everything else.
"Retries are safe only because the call carries an idempotency key."
BackoffWait longer each retry: 100, 200, 400 ms.
JitterRandom extra wait, so clients don't retry in sync.
Circuit breakerClosed, then open (fail fast), then half-open (test one call).
BulkheadSeparate connection pools per service, like a ship's compartments.

Go-live

19

Flags and kill switches

Settings you flip while the site runs, with no code release. A kill switch is a flag for emergencies.

Light switches you install before the party.
Pause ordersWaiting room onHide photosClose a slot
Scenario. 4:10 PM on game day: payment errors spike.
Without it
  1. Write code, review, release, mid-incident
  2. 20+ minutes of failed checkouts
With it
  1. Flip 'pause new orders'
  2. Seconds later the button says 'paused for a few minutes'
  3. Paid orders keep flowing; flip back at 4:25
  4. Database busy later? Flip 'hide photos'
Why it works: A switch takes seconds, and each one was tested the day before.
"Every switch is tested on game day."
Feature flagTurns a feature on or off at runtime.
Graceful degradationTurn off extras so the core keeps working.
Load sheddingReject some requests instantly so the rest stay fast.
20

Load test and game day

A load test fires fake traffic at 2 to 3x the peak at a test copy, with a pass bar. A game day rehearses: flip switches, fake outages.

A dress rehearsal before opening night.
Before every launch
Scenario. Two days before a doorbuster sale.
Without it
  1. A missing index is found at 6 AM on sale day
  2. On-call has never practiced pausing orders
With it
  1. k6 simulates 120,000 visitors, 1,000 buying one TV
  2. Pass bar: 50 sold per deal, 0 double charges, p95 under 500 ms
  3. Run 1 fails (missing index); fix, rerun, pass
  4. Game day: flip every switch, fake Stripe errors, time the response
Why it works: You find the missing index two days early, not at 6 AM on sale day.
"Pass means 0 oversold, 0 double charges, and p95 under 500 ms."
StagingThe test copy of your system.
k6, LocustLoad-testing tools.
Chaos testingBreaking parts on purpose. Game day is the small version.
Capacity planningYour Clarify math, written down.
21

Safe deploys

Release new code to a few users first, keep a tested way back, and stop releasing around the event.

Taste the soup before serving the whole room.
Any release near the event
Scenario. Wednesday before the snowy weekend: the tire shop ships a bug fix.
Without it
  1. The fix goes to 100% at once
  2. A bad release breaks booking for everyone
With it
  1. Canary: 5% of traffic gets the new version
  2. Errors on that 5% jump from 0.1% to 4%: one-click rollback
  3. Proper fix Thursday morning, then freeze until Monday
Why it works: Most outages come from changes. A canary limits who, a rollback limits how long, a freeze removes the risk.
"Nothing ships during the window."
CanaryNew version to 1 to 5% of users first.
RollbackA tested, one-step return to the last version.
Code freezeNo releases around the event.
Rolling deployReplace copies a few at a time; can kill work in progress.
Blue-greenOld and new side by side; switch all traffic at once.
22

Dashboards and alerts

Live charts of the system's health, plus automatic pages to the on-call person when a number crosses a line.

A car dashboard, with warning lights.
Launch dayEvery day after
Scenario. Game day, 3:40 PM: 'held' orders climb, but 'paid' goes flat.
Without it
  1. Only errors and latency watched: both look normal
  2. Customers can't pay, and nobody knows
With it
  1. Alert: held minus paid above 50 for 3 min
  2. It pages the on-call engineer
  3. Runbook: check Stripe's status; if it's down, pause orders
Why it works: The right number tells on-call what's wrong, not just that something is.
"If errors pass 2% for 2 minutes, I turn on the waiting room."
Golden signalsLatency, traffic, errors, saturation (how full).
Business metricsOrders a minute, held vs paid, stock left.
p9595% of requests are faster than this. Shows the slow tail.
RunbookA written 'if this fires, do this', with an owner.
Logs, tracesEvent records, and one request followed step by step.
SLOThe target, like 99.9% of checkouts under 1 s.
23

Security basics

Five things in one breath: HTTPS, secrets in a vault, the provider handles cards, least privilege, little personal data.

Lock the doors, keep the keys in a safe, let the bank hold the cash.
When asked
Scenario. The interviewer asks: 'What about security?'
Weak answer
  1. 10 minutes on encryption algorithms
  2. No time left for go-live
Strong answer
  1. HTTPS everywhere
  2. Stripe key and DB password in a secrets manager
  3. No card numbers, thanks to hosted checkout
  4. Text worker can read the queue, not the database
  5. Store only a name and phone number
Why it works: It shows you know the basics, in 20 seconds.
"HTTPS everywhere, secrets in a vault, no card data on our servers, and each service gets only what it needs."
AuthenticationProving who you are.
AuthorizationWhat you're allowed to do.
Least privilegeEach part gets only what it needs.

Numbers to know

1M events a day
about 12 a second on average; the busiest hour is 2 to 3x that
One row
about 1 KB, so 1M rows is about 1 GB: "it all fits in memory"
One Postgres database
thousands of simple writes a second
Postgres connections
a few hundred at most, so use a pooler (card 15)
Redis, one server
about 100,000 reads or writes a second, each under 1 ms
One API copy
hundreds to a couple of thousand requests a second
Autoscaling
reacts in 1 to 5 minutes
A payment call
200 ms to over 1 s; Stripe allows about 100 requests a second by default
