System design interview framework

TLDR. Six phases in 60 minutes. Ask questions until you have numbers. Build the simplest version that works. Give a reason and a rejected alternative for every choice. Add spike defenses cheapest first. Spend real time on go-live. Finish with a summary and the trade-offs.

Keep 2 minutes of buffer for the interviewer's questions. If a phase runs more than 3 minutes over, say so and move on: "I'll leave V1 there so we have time for the spike."

The habits that get scored

  1. Talk the whole time. Silence reads as stuck. Think out loud: "I'm deciding between X and Y. X is simpler, so..."
  2. Signpost. Start each phase by naming it: "Now version one, the simple design for normal load."
  3. Check in. Every 5 to 10 minutes: "Does this match what you had in mind, or should I go deeper anywhere?" The interviewer often steers you toward what they grade.
  4. A reason for every choice. Never just "I'd use Postgres." Say "Postgres, not DynamoDB, because..."
  5. Don't over-engineer. If you add a component, say what problem it solves at these numbers. If it doesn't solve one yet, leave it out or keep it behind a flag.
  6. Use numbers. "Ten writes a second fits on one database," not "this scales."
  7. Bring your own experience in. One real story per interview is worth more than any amount of theory (see "Your stories" below).

Phase 1: Clarify (0 to 6 min)

Goal: turn a vague ask into numbers, find the hard limit, and name what must never break.

Open with: "Before I design anything, I'll spend about five minutes on questions."

Question bank (ask 5 to 7, not all)

Users and flow
Ask
Who uses it? Web, app, both? What's the one core action?
Why it matters
Defines the endpoints and the core write
Normal traffic
Ask
What's a normal day or hour?
Why it matters
Your baseline for V1
The spike
Ask
How big, how fast does it arrive, how long does it last? (10x for 2 hours is a different problem from 1,000x for 5 minutes)
Why it matters
Decides whether autoscaling is enough or you must pre-scale
Hard limit
Ask
Is there a fixed supply? (seats, ovens, units, slots)
Why it matters
Often caps the writes, so the spike becomes a read problem
Must never fail
Ask
What would be a disaster? (oversell, double charge, lost order, lost booking)
Why it matters
Becomes your invariants, protected in the database
May degrade
Ask
What can be slow or missing for an hour? (photos, emails, status page)
Why it matters
Tells you what to put on a queue or switch off
Team, time, budget
Ask
How many engineers, how long, what's already in place? (payments, cloud)
Why it matters
Justifies keeping it simple
Read vs write
Ask
Are users mostly looking or mostly acting?
Why it matters
Picks the cache and DB strategy
Geography
Ask
One city, one country, global?
Why it matters
Usually one region is fine; say so

Then do the math out loud

  • Peak reads per second = visitors at the peak minute / 60 x calls per visit.
  • Peak writes per second = actions at the peak minute / 60. Cap it by the hard limit.
  • Data size = rows x about 1 KB. Usually tiny, so say "the data fits in memory."
  • One-line conclusion: "So the spike is a read problem, and the write path is small but has to be correct."

Common mistakes: asking questions without turning the answers into numbers; skipping "what must never fail"; spending 12 minutes here.

Phase 2: Version 1 (6 to 20 min)

Goal: the simplest design that works at normal load, with one request traced end to end.

Say: "Version one is the simple design. I'll get one order working end to end, then harden it for the spike."

The default V1 (start here, change only with a reason)

Browser
↓
CDNstatic site, images
↓
Load balancer
↓
API service2+ stateless instances
↓
Postgres (managed)
Third partyStripe, SMS, webhook back
Scheduled jobsweeper, reports

Cover these four things

  1. Boxes and arrows. Client, CDN, load balancer, API, one database, any third party, any background job.
  2. Endpoints. 4 to 6 of them. Mark the one that writes, and give it an idempotency key.
  3. Schema. The 2 or 3 core tables, with their key columns, a status field, and unique constraints.
  4. One request end to end. Walk the core write step by step, including the transaction and what happens on failure.

Say it out loud

"At about N writes a second and a few GB of data, this fits on one database with room to spare." Interviewers listen for this sentence because it shows you can tell when you don't need to scale.

Common mistakes: drawing 12 boxes before one request works; putting in Kafka, microservices or sharding at 10 writes a second; forgetting the schema.

Phase 4: Spike defenses (26 to 40 min)

Goal: survive the spike without breaking the invariants. Add defenses cheapest first, and say which one you'd skip.

Say: "Now the spike. I'll add defenses cheapest first."

1 CDN for static content
Protects
API, bandwidth
Note
Free win. Cache the menu or catalog JSON for 30 to 60 s too
2 Cache the read paths (short TTL)
Protects
DB reads
Note
1 to 5 s for availability; it's a hint, the DB write is the truth
3 Waiting room or queue in front of checkout
Protects
Everything behind it
Note
Often keep it behind a flag; turn on only if traffic beats the plan
4 Rate limits per user and per IP
Protects
Fairness, bots, retry storms
Note
At the edge (WAF) plus a business limit (2 per phone)

Protects

Note

Protects

7 Async queue for non-urgent work
Protects

Note
Emails, SMS, receipts, analytics
8 Connection limits and third-party limits
Protects
DB, payment provider

Connection pooler; call the provider only after a hold succeeds

Follow-ups to expect (and the answer shape)

"The payment provider is slow or down."
The hold already protects inventory. Timeout plus retry with the same idempotency key. Kill switch to pause new orders. Reconcile with the provider before releasing a hold.
"The spike is 10x bigger. What breaks first?"
Name the first thing to fail and why (usually the read path or DB connections, not the capped writes). Then the defense: CDN, cache, waiting room on.
"The database goes down."
Managed failover (about 30 to 60 s), retries on the client, the waiting room absorbs it. RPO and RTO from backups.
"The cache goes down."
Reads fall through to the DB. Protect it with a connection cap and load shedding. Never let the cache be the source of truth.
"A webhook arrives twice, or late."
Dedupe on event id. The order status only moves forward. Reconcile job for late ones.
"Bots buy everything."
Rate limits, one per account or phone, CAPTCHA at the waiting room (vendor), and cancel abusive orders afterward.
"One hot item or key."
It's a single row: a conditional update serializes on it. That's fine at 10/s. At 1,000/s, split the counter into N buckets or pre-allocate in batches.
"How do you know it's working?"
Name the 3 or 4 metrics you'd watch (see go-live).

Phase 5: Go-live prep (40 to 52 min)

Goal: the part most candidates skip, and often what interviewers care about most. Be concrete: numbers, dates, pass criteria.

Say: "Go-live prep. This is where launches fail, so I'll be specific."

1 Load test at 2 to 3x peak
Make it concrete
Tool (k6, Locust), environment (staging, provider in test mode), pass bar: 0 oversold, 0 double charges, p95 < 500 ms
2 Pre-scale
Make it concrete
"At T minus 1 hour, scale to N instances, bump the DB tier, warm the CDN." Autoscaling takes minutes; the spike takes seconds
3 Feature flags and kill switches
Make it concrete
List them: pause orders, waiting room on, hide heavy features, close a slot. Test each one
4 Staged rollout with tested rollback
Make it concrete
Canary before the freeze. Code freeze before the event. No deploys during the window
5 Dashboards and alerts
Make it concrete
Errors, p95 latency, queue depth, inventory left, held vs paid, provider errors, DB connections
6 Runbook, on-call, game day
Make it concrete
Owner per alert, a written "if X then Y", and a dry run: load test, flip every switch, fake a provider outage
7 Raise third-party limits
Make it concrete
Ask the payment or SMS provider to raise rate limits ahead of time

"It's go-live morning. What are you watching?"

  • Before: pre-scale done, CDN hit rate high, flags in the right state, on-call present.
  • During: error rate, p95 latency, queue depth, held vs paid (a gap means payments are failing), inventory left vs the real world.
  • The trigger: "If X crosses Y, I flip Z." For example, error rate over 2% for 2 minutes turns on the waiting room.

Phase 6: Wrap-up (52 to 58 min)

Summary (30 seconds): the bottleneck, how the invariants are protected, how the spike is absorbed, how go-live is de-risked.

Trade-offs (3 or 4): what you knowingly gave up. Examples:

  • Cached availability can be stale for a few seconds, so some users learn "sold out" at checkout.
  • Holds lock inventory for N minutes even if unpaid.
  • One region and one database: managed failover, not multi-region.
  • Hosted checkout: less control over the payment page.

What I'd do next with more time: one or two items, such as multi-region or a better fairness system.

Prompt archetypes

Most "small business plus spike" prompts are one of these. Recognize the shape early.

Limited inventory drop
Examples
Pizza promo, flash-sale doorbusters, sneaker drop
Invariant
No oversell, no double charge
Key trick
Conditional decrement plus expiring holds; waiting room; reads are the spike
Write-heavy counter
Examples
Likes, reactions, step-challenge leaderboards
Invariant
Each event counted once, none lost
Key trick
Send totals instead of deltas where you can; otherwise append events to a queue or log and count async; dedupe per user; show approximate totals
Job burst
Examples
Photo processing before a deadline, video encoding, report generation
Invariant
No job lost, none run twice
Key trick
Queue plus a worker pool; concurrency caps; retries with idempotency; backpressure; priority lanes
Reservations
Examples
Restaurant tables, appointments, classes
Invariant
No double booking
Key trick
Unique constraint on (resource, time slot); holds with expiry
Notifications fan-out
Examples
Flash-sale alert to 1M users
Invariant
Delivered at most once, eventually
Key trick
Queue, batch sends, provider rate limits, retries with backoff

Your stories (use one per interview)

Map one real reliability story from your own work to each phase. Keep each to two sentences: what happened, and what you changed. Prepare them before the interview so you are not inventing one under pressure.

Red flags (these get a no-hire)

  • Drawing the "Netflix architecture" (Kafka, microservices, sharding) for a small business.
  • No numbers anywhere.
  • No answer to "why this and not that."
  • Never mentioning how you stop overselling or double charging.
  • Ending at the diagram, with nothing about load testing, rollout or monitoring.
  • Long silences, or not answering the question that was asked.
